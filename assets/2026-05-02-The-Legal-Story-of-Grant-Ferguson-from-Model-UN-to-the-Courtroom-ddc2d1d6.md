“Martingrove students stand out. It’s a very special school.” This is proven by the fact that we have produced standout alumni like Mr. Grant Ferguson, a lawyer with Stieber Berlach focusing on insurance defence litigation who was generous enough to host me at his office for an interview. Despite briefly getting lost in the elevator, I finally sat down to interview him about his time at Martingrove, which began in 1990.

  

At MCI, Mr. Ferguson most enjoyed science and math classes, especially with the extra challenges offered by the Gifted program (the AP program wasn’t in place at that time). “Martingrove was always very academically strong,” he notes, with exceptional teachers that push students to think outside the box and find their own interests.

  

As well, our diversity of clubs has always been a staple of MCI, offering students lots of leadership opportunities in his time and in mine. The myriad of experiences offered by Martingrove gives students the chance to shine as leaders not only in their high school years, but also in their professional futures, something that Mr. Ferguson has experienced firsthand. He credits the Martingrove Model United Nations with giving him his first exposure to litigation, which any past delegate knows is a mild way to describe the events of MMUN, and his involvement in debate society and orchestra with developing his leadership skills.

  

Having enjoyed high school science classes, Mr. Ferguson attended the University of Toronto, inspired by his many medical family members to study nursing, with a goal in mind: medical school. However, in his last year, he switched into pharmacology, which he found was a better fit for him. But, after writing the MCAT, “it didn’t feel right,” he said, about accepting his offer to med school at that point, so he decided to take a gap year.

  

Not wanting to move out of the city and uproot his family, he decided to follow his wife’s suggestion of writing the LSAT. He described the notorious test as “fun, like a puzzle game,” which gave me no surprise that he was accepted into the University of Toronto’s law school, which at that time was a smaller program that still allowed him to stay in the city.

  

Studying at U of T gave Mr. Ferguson the opportunity to hone his legal skills at the Downtown Legal Services clinic, giving him practice in all different areas of law. From his experiences with criminal law, he found he enjoyed being in court, but didn’t like the other elements of criminal law so much, so he applied to a mix of government and litigation firms for his articling position (a 10-month apprenticeship that newly graduated lawyers undergo before they are licensed). He completed this term at Stieber Berlach and has worked there ever since. “Every day is different, and it’s never boring,” Mr. Ferguson explains. His day-to-day activities can include drafting and reviewing documents; meetings with clients, counsel, or court attendants; or teaching a fourth-year moot court class at U of T, which he has done for the past 15 years.  He particularly enjoys the detail-oriented aspects and the “agree to disagree” mentality required for a lawyer to succeed.

  

When asked, he says there’s nothing in his past that he would have done differently, if given the chance. “Mistakes inform you about who you are, and they ultimately help you learn lessons.” Mr. Ferguson’s non traditional path to law ended up being a benefit for him in his current position, which goes to show that even mistakes can be spun into advantages if you hold the right mindset. You might find yourself doing unpleasant things, or things you don’t enjoy, but make sure that you have a clear goal in mind at the end.

  

To current students, he stresses the importance of taking up challenging experiences, building your character and skills for your future. However, you must also recognize that efficiency and the classic ideal of success doesn’t necessarily lead to happiness for all people. In his life, he strives to make room for things he enjoys, like travelling and spending time with his family.

  

“Be a tree, not a machine.” This is Mr. Ferguson’s way of saying that true success comes from working hard to achieve your goals, but also taking time to enjoy the fruits of your labours, something that he hopes all students realize. I hope everyone can connect with some of his words of wisdom, which I am grateful that he shared!

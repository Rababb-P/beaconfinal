**Upon finishing my interview with Mr. Mark Ellis, a writer, TV producer, and MCI alum, I felt hopeful for my future as an artist.** As someone who has unfortunately followed the “art kid doing STEM” road, I have been left thinking about how art will fit into my life when school inevitably starts to take over. Along with that, I’ve been picturing what my life would be like had I chosen my hobby as a full time career, and wondering whether pursuing your passions full-time ends with you being unable to enjoy them. But having an insight on Mr. Ellis’ life has not only made me rethink all of this, but has also provided me an insight into what artistic paths can look like.

  

I began by asking him about some of his well-known projects, *Flashpoint* and *X Company*, co-created by him and his partner Stephanie Morgenstern. As a showrunner, he is responsible for the artistic production of the show, and oversees basically everything. From the hiring of actors, designers and editors, to post-production, he takes care of it all. That amount of organization alone is amazing, but he also mentioned how he works pretty closely on the plot of the show, creating the concepts, overarching seasons, and character arcs. “It’s a huge job, it’s not just about the writing.” But, of course, the job can’t just be done all by one person. His partner, and the talented group of people he co-leads allows for such a heavy job to be done properly, and with little to no problems.

  

Most people in high school don’t think that their time here will be valuable, but according to Mr. Ellis, Martingrove was very valuable to him, in part thanks to the extracurricular activities offered here. One thing MCI Bears will always do is join way too many clubs. Mr. Ellis was a part of the Drama Club (now known as Martingrove Stage Company), and was former Editor-In-Chief of the MCI Beacon (shoutout Beacon!).

  

Through these clubs, he had the opportunity to meet students that shared his passions, something he continued to seek in university, and even in professional collaborations now. “There’s an opportunity for creating safe spaces to explore ideas together when you get along.” According to him, using this idea of rooting yourself in collaboration will take you far in a professional setting, especially in the entertainment industry. High school allows for these roots to form, as the ideas you and others are exploring have no bearing on your career, they are solely passion projects coming straight from you. He also mentioned how MCI’s staff, namely Mr. James Hunt, fostered a great environment for him and his peers to push themselves outside of their comfort zone, and see just how far their ideas can go. I guess nothing has really changed at Martingrove.

  

This collaboration has continued in his professional life, and has now become the highlight of his current job. As a former actor himself, Mr. Ellis enjoys the acting-writing alliance, and seeing how both sides begin to take the other’s ideas and mannerisms into account. He particularly mentioned how he enjoys writing a character’s dialogue with the knowledge of how the actor would say it, and creating a personalized script with that knowledge. These personal connections are also especially present between writers. Having close connections can create a great writer’s room full of ideas that will serve the story being written, which is quite necessary as the job is very fast-paced. During production, Mr. Ellis is working 7 days a week, 12 hours a day, which can seem really hard for some people, but he enjoys the quickness as he is able to see all the work he has been doing brought to the screen fairly quickly.

  

As a final question, I asked him about what advice he had for current MCI students. He reminded me that every pathway looks different, and even if you can’t get into the program you want, or decide you hate the it a few years down the line, as long as you continue to pursue something in the field you truly do want, you’ll eventually find your way.

  

For him, that looked like doing a lot of non-union television and theatre acting, which allowed him to still work with professionals and get his foot in the door, eventually leading to him getting a writing agent and being able to pursue writing through getting picked up by CTV. “The older I get, the more I believe a career is not a straight line, I think it’s a path that can take different turns and different directions.” If you continue to expose yourself to different forms of art, artists can teach you things that can eventually lead to your full-time career.

  

“Keeping your eyes and ears open to the world and to the art that’s out there is really important.” As the interview came to a close, Mr. Ellis gave a final bit of advice, which is to keep seeing the art that is out there. We live in such a rich community, full of so much art if you just stop and look for it. Buy tickets to your local theatre production. Seek out art shows and local concerts (the Toronto music scene is magnificent). But even if all of this is inaccessible for you, there are always ways to find art. Search up TV scripts online and dissect them, create your own 3-minute movie, write pages of poetry. Even if you don’t want to pursue art full time, finding like-minded people where you can share your artwork with them will keep art in your life, whether it be in high school or outside in this big wide world.

  

If there’s one thing I took away from my conversation with Mr. Ellis, it’s that life is so much larger than whatever is happening in high school right now. My future career is so beyond my control that the only way I can even influence it is if I just keep pursuing my passions, so that’s what I’m planning on doing. Thank you so much, Mr. Mark Ellis, for all the lovely advice and insight into your life. It was a true pleasure.

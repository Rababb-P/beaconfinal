*Anton Ego, known for his vicious and unforgiving critiques on the global food scene, has been employed by The Beacon in the absence of its resident food critic, **Ethan Lin**, after being temporarily suspended for **chronic deadline tardiness.** Once hailed as France’s most influential, Ego held the nickname of ‘Grim Eater’ until his empire’s collapse as a result of his scandalous 2007 review entitled ‘France’s finest’ of the now-perished Gusteau’s in Paris. Almost 20 years later, he returns to his craft, most adventurous and excited, but also more damning than ever. All the more remains his infamous motto: “I don't ‘like’ food. I ‘love’ it. If I don't love it, I don't swallow.”*

  

**When I choose to indulge in ethnic cuisine, I typically entrust the old-school institutions to soothe my cravings.** One seldom finds a “hip” or “modern” establishment capable of serving a bowl of noodles with even an ounce of integrity, a smudge of character, or a lick of passion. I certainly cannot—or rather, I could not. Not until I gave the people of Bao House a chance, albeit reluctantly. Ultimately, even though my expectations were subterranean, I cannot say that my reaction to the meal would have been altered in the slightest by hopeful anticipation.

  

The dining room atmosphere of Bao House (whose name I shall refrain from commenting on for being so uninspiringly on-the-nose) challenges the traditional colour palette of the classic Chinese restaurant. The restaurant glows brightly, as the orange and yellow hues emit an intense aura—too much for my  liking. But I thought to myself that, perhaps, the radiant tones were a symbol of the staff’s passion for their craft? “How charming,” I thought.

  

It must be noted that the aromas which emerge from this building had perfumed Yonge Street so richly that I suspect I would have been intrigued enough to go inside and investigate the restaurant even if I had not been conducting work. The euphoric smells only thrilled my nostrils more vividly as the various dishes began settling upon my table. This reality violated all my expectations, but I had never in my life been more delighted to be wrong.

  

The dish of choice that evening, a house noodle with fried mince, posed no visual threat, which I acknowledged as an intelligent play, because should it disappoint, it would at least yield no pretensions. With that said, like all noodle dishes of its kind, the presentation reaches its artistic climax as the customer takes their chopsticks and gloriously combines the noodles with the *accoutrement.* This was no exception. I wasted no more time. Right then and there marked the gustatory climax of the whole meal, where the dish sang an exquisite harmony resonant of savour and spice, where no flavour overshadowed another, and where all my taste senses rang at a frequency capable of shattering my ceramic bowl. For the first time in forever, I was truly astounded.



Ever since beginning the practice in the mid-70s, I quickly learned that I would spend most of life feeling *disappointed*. Generally speaking, this was not some erroneous life lesson of mine, having wasted too much priceless energy on underwhelming bites, chews, and swallows. The failed restaurateurs that attempt to challenge and even critique *me* try to delude  their associates into thinking that *I* am the one with the twisted moral compass—believe me, anyone who dares to serve what those people serve are the truly twisted, and their associates know it too (they are **failed** restaurateurs, after all).

  

In the minds of seasoned critics like myself, we often fall dumb to our memories of a time that once was—perhaps our childhoods, or just any simpler time—so much so that we find ourselves judging the living hell out of artists trying their hands at something for the first time. Evidently, the authentic legacy of Chinese cuisine has been flawlessly transferred over by the elders who taught these young chefs so meticulously, regardless of their building’s pompously incandescent colour scheme. I have reached a point in my life where my body has reached a limit on the amount of mediocre dishes that it can tolerate. It took time to accustom myself to a world overabundant with vacuity. But it is a big world out there, and every now and then, I am confronted to reconsider my own philosophies. Sometimes, taking a chance is worth it—you might never know until you try.

**Lindsay Zier-Vogel is an award-winning writer and educator.** Her most recent novel, The Fun Times Brigade follows Amy, a new mom balancing motherhood, grief, and her music career, as she explores what success and family mean to her. Zier-Vogel also wrote the Forest of Reading Blue Spruce nominee picture book Dear Street and novel Letters To Amelia, which tie into her Love Lettering Project.

  

The Love Lettering Project is an international program in which participants write love letters to their neighbourhoods meant to foster community. Here, I ask Lindsay about her own community building, as well as her creative method and the beginning of her writing career here at MCI. This interview has been edited for length and clarity.

  

**Penny: Your Love Letters project and its focus on being tied to the community resonated with me. Could you tell me about how growing up in the area/attending MCI has shaped your work?**

  

Lindsay: I actually grew up far from MCI, so I would bike 8 km to school. My close friends didn’t live nearby and I felt this lack of a neighbourhood community growing up, so I cultivated it as an adult. In my 20s, my community was something that really mattered to me. I would throw dinner parties and bring people together. I think that focusing on placemaking, citybuilding, and awareness of what’s happening in the community was always important to me.

  

**P: Moving to a new place, or even returning home after a long time can be isolating experiences. Do you have any advice for building a circle around you from scratch, so to speak?**

  

L: My advice based on my experiences is to find people who are really excited about the same things you’re excited about. I used to bind books and make zines, and I would participate in book and zine fairs. I ended up having this amazing community of people who loved similar stuff. I wasn’t just saying “I like books and reading” because that's so big and broad. What worked was explaining that “I like this very specific thing about writing or reading”. I think that sports can cultivate that a lot as well, even though I personally didn’t play a lot of team sports. I also got into fiber arts. I would go to knit nights in public spaces to meet people. Do what you want to do and also find people who are willing to do that specific thing. We’re very rarely alone in the things we love.

  

**P: Essentially, you should put yourself out there to find a community with shared interests?**

  

L: Yes. I have this writing group. We meet every month and share our writing, and we’ve been writing together for around 8 years now. That is a very long tenure for a writing group. It started because one of my closest friends emailed me and some other writers saying “Hey, do you want to do this?”. It was such a small ask, and any one of us could have said no, but we all said yes. The stakes weren't high. I think that’s the key. The first step shouldn’t feel too big. You can’t just create a “capital C community” out of nothing. You start by connecting with one person, and then that person connects with another person. A very small step can build an entire community.

  

**P: It’s easy to get out of the habit of being creative and pursuing passions like writing and art. What do you use to find motivation to create?**

  

L: First of all, I know that by making art or writing, I feel like a more engaged human in the world, I feel more myself. I have a few tricks as well. One is that I have a writing group. I want to get stuff written so I can share it with them. Making those external deadlines creates a helpful structure in which I have to produce work. In terms of the day-to-day, I have a mason jar filled with paper slips. I think about all the ideas or scenes I want to write, and they go on slips into the jar. Every morning, before my kids wake up, I sit down for 20 minutes. I pull out a piece of paper and make myself write. I always have 20 minutes, it’s a very manageable amount of time. I’m giving myself tasks, since I’m good at following directions. I get out of my own way and avoid just staring at a blank screen, which I find very helpful. By the time I’m done writing a full jar of ideas, I have enough raw material to experiment with and layer in different ways. Rather than asking myself “I want to write a novel. Where do I start?”, I can say “I want to write a novel and here are 50 Word documents that I can use to get started.”

  

**P: You’ve made the first step smaller.**

  

L: Exactly, I’m all about very small first steps. I think it’s really easy to get overwhelmed by the first step and give up.

  

**P: I just finished your book The Fun Times Brigade, and I enjoyed the music aspect of it. I've always been interested in the stories of musicians and bands. Are there any songs that remind you of high school? Did any of them make it into your books?**

  

L: That’s such a good question. I listened to a lot of Ani Di Franco and 60s-70s rock. That was definitely in the Fun Times Brigade, like the Guess Who, Neil Young. Ani has not made it into any of my books so far. The one I’m working on right now might have some references though.

  

**P: For anyone who wants to be an encouraging figure to a young writer in their life, what would you recommend?**

  

L: Find out what they are interested in, and get them books that will spark their imagination. Profound encouragement is very valuable for them. I became a writer because of Ms. Salgo. She was a teacher at Martingrove who taught Grade 9 English and Writer’s Craft. Her belief in my writing and my voice really carried me for a long time. Writers get a lot of rejections, and to have someone so completely and absolutely believe in my voice from such a young age was a true gift. That was really foundational to my growth.

  

**P: Thanks so much, this interview was really insightful. I’m looking forward to getting into some more writing!**

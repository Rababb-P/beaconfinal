**There’s something about urban photography that draws me in.** Most urban spaces in North America weren’t designed with aesthetics in mind, making a good shot difficult to come by. A never-ending housing crisis in Toronto has prompted the rise of cookie-cutter suburbs and copy-and-paste apartment buildings, purging the city of character. The spots that are worth shooting have been captured thousands of times before. Shooting good, original urban photography challenges photographers to create something beautiful out of something ordinary. It requires as little as yourself, a smartphone, and the eye of a photographer—a skill you can learn. With the help of photos shot by Daniel Mok, I will lead you through the process and provide tips that can help you become a better urban photographer.  

  

**Shooting with Intent**  

  

One helpful tip when shooting in urban spaces is to use the straight edges of buildings as leading lines.  

  The roofs of the buildings on both sides of the road direct your attention to the bridge, producing a much more interesting composition than a shot of the bridge alone. Other geometric patterns common in cities can also work to your advantage. The arch under the bridge frames a distant skyscraper, highlighting an otherwise insignificant landmark. Shooting with intent means looking for these patterns and finding a spot where one or more align. No single object in the photo is captivating, yet the product as a whole draws you in.  

    
**Lighting and Space**  

  
The CN Tower is a classic example of an appealing building shot far too often. However, creative lighting and space choices help turn this repetitive composition into a stunning product. In this shot, the sun shines towards the camera. The back lighting casts a silhouette on the figure and the skyscraper, creating an intense contrast with the sky. Shooting close up and from below adds lots of negative space, complementing the lighting by capturing the entire gradient effect from the sunset.  

  

  **Human Connection**  

    
At first glance, this photo is not nearly as interesting as the previous two. There is no distinct composition, contrasting colours, or unique perspectives. The road is shot slightly off-centre, but it appears to be an intentional choice to keep the car turning right in the frame. There is no obvious subject; but if there were, would you have noticed the scooterist creeping between the lanes, or the cars swerving out of their lanes as if to ask, “what’s taking so long?” These small details give a seemingly simple photo a subtle charm. Never ignore the human aspect while shooting—urban photography is as much a still frame as a medium for storytelling.
The next time you’re surrounded by high rises or on a bustling street, I challenge you to pull out a camera and shoot. With practice and intent, you can transform monotonous urban spaces into remarkable works of art—one frame at a time.

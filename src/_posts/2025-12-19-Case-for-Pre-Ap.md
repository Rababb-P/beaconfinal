**“No one prepared me for this.”**  The sentiment reverberates across Grade 11 classrooms, and while the transition from sophomore year is certainly a difficult one, there is another factor to consider. 2023 marked the first year since Pre-AP courses in grades 9/10 were removed from MCI’s AP program, making the class of 2027 the first generation of students to be offered only two choices, destreamed/academic or gifted, in 9/10 core subjects. While the rationale behind this decision is an admirable one—open doors to academic pathways are integral to a more equitable education system—there is a conversation to be had when considering its impact on students’ preparedness and achievement in the face of more demanding course loads.
I spoke to current Grade 11s to gain some insight into their experiences. It wasn’t long before I noticed a clear consensus: those taking Pre-AP classes for the first time feel particularly overwhelmed, citing a lack of exposure to material and inadequate preparation in earlier grades. “It’s like we did nothing last year, and now I’m drowning,” expressed a student taking Pre-AP Math & Biology. Another remarked that “there’s just so much work [compared to before], the teachers expect more, and I feel like I’m the only one who can’t keep up.” Many also feel frustrated that while they were “twiddling [their] thumbs” in an understimulating destreamed classroom, their peers in gifted courses were privy to the rigour of Pre-AP since the very beginning. “You guys [gifted] read Hemingway?” asked a stressed out student in the midst of preparing to write a formal literary essay for “like, the second time ever.” I was most saddened to hear how many echoed deep regret, insisting that they “should’ve dropped” their higher level classes, and hope that they persist beyond their doubts.  

  

Allow me to introduce the analogy of a swimming lesson: imagine a group of children of vastly different abilities all developing their swimming skills. In a destreamed/academic class, the instructor must contend with this entire spectrum, and will naturally have to devote much of their energy and attention to those just learning to swim, leaving everyone to doggy paddle in shallow waters even if some can handle the deep end. Or, maybe the lesson focuses on an intermediate level, leaving some drowning and others still bored. Conversely, an AP class is more akin to swimming drills in the depths of an Olympic pool, which fosters elevated achievement by challenging swimmers to the max. Translated to the junior Pre-AP classroom, it is evident as to why smart and capable students feel out of their depth without previous exposure to a higher standard; it’s not that they can’t do the butterfly stroke, it’s just that they’ve only really had the chance to practice front crawl.  

  
I propose a straightforward solution: instead of delaying entry into the AP program, why not make it more flexible, empowering students to choose a pathway that works for them without closing any doors early on? An open, interest-based approach to 9-11 Pre-AP courses, with prerequisites remaining only for Grade 12 APs, would allow MCI students to opt in to more challenging classes at almost any point, sans entrance criteria or pressure. This would provide vital preparation and adequate stimulation tailored to the individual, and perhaps even remove misconceptions about the program’s perceived difficulty that may unnecessarily dissuade students from taking AP classes. My hope is that we can ensure equity not through the one size fits all framework that may result from destreaming, but via diverse course offerings that support various needs and pathways, such as Pre-AP. Let us imagine a world where we can all learn how to swim freely, both in the shallow and the deep end.

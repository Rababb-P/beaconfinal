**“Top 10 hacks to glow up for summer!” exclaims the bubbly Lululemon-clad woman on my screen.** Her video is just one of millions that methodically swarm our feeds, fueling a buzzing undercurrent that never fails to remind us of the ways we fail ourselves, our bodies, and the people we hope to become. Tamping down the disquiet in my mind, I find myself fascinated by the peak seasons of self improvement mania—when we look past the fall frenzy to return to school better (looking) and the semi-irony of the ‘winter arc’ as a precursor to forgotten New Year’s promises, it all seems to cycle back to summer. The perfect summer is not defined by exciting vacations, ice cream on a hot day, or even just sunshine warming our faces. Instead, this elusive success is contingent on how good we may look sprawled on a beach towel, which in turn discourages us from picking up the ice cream cone.  

  The annual wave of workout challenges, glow up tips, and diet trends is not accidental.
Influencers, companies, and anyone looking to sell something are quick to capitalize off our collective panic as the days until summer tick down and we still have not achieved our goals. We are consumed by what we consume, whether that be food or the content that touts the dangers of eating it. In an unsurprising resurgence of early noughts “thin is in”, this year’s iteration is even more openly prioritizing thin bodies and flat stomachs, the implications of which are alarming for vulnerable populations. Young people, women, and of course those at the intersection of teenage girlhood are easy targets for this algorithm-aided propaganda that pretends to boost their self esteem only to further degrade it.
It’s insidious, really. Language designed to encourage us to become the best versions of ourselves—“lock in!”—is co-opted to remind us that appearance trumps all other areas for improvement. According to this messaging, the “best” me is hot before she is intelligent; beautiful before she is kind. By presenting the pursuit of a perfect body as a simple matter of discipline, influencers incorrectly conflate morality or social value with beauty, often under a facade of faux-empowerment and corporate-speak guilt tripping. Not that these ideas are new—they’ve just assumed another medium and found yet another way of keeping our consciences hostage.  

  I’m angry, and I’m tired. The incessant cycle of chasing shinier, prettier, and cooler versions of ourselves inevitably results in disappointment, and it’s exhausting to juggle a wavering self image with the images of perfection we aspire to. Here is my proposition: Instead of resolving to another Sisyphean summer glow up, let’s log out, step off the scale, away from the mirror, and far from the carousel that reduces our worth to superficial snapshots. It’s not a crime to seek transformation; I am simply urging us all to focus our efforts on more permanent endeavours and to find meaning in who we are beyond what we look like. Sometimes, it’s best to forget the fit of your swimsuit and focus on the scoop of vanilla melting in the July sun.

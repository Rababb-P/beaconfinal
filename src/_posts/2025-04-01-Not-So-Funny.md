**“You can’t cancel me, I’m not your gym membership,”** proclaims Matt Rife to thundering applause. In the wake of his callous attitude about domestic abuse and subsequent criticism, the Tiktok-famous young comedian has joined ranks with other comics whose jokes, also perceived as offensive, have sparked controversy. Dabbling in taboo topics to elicit laughter, comedy is a prominent battleground for culture wars, especially in the Internet-enabled era of Twitter outrage. Let’s don our thinking caps.  

  

These comedians seem thoroughly undecided; are they free-speech crusaders fighting back against cancellation and promoting profound intellectualism, or are they simply funnymen whose ideas shouldn’t be taken at face value because they’re “just joking”? Dave Chappelle, another eager lieutenant against cancel culture following his controversial transphobic segment, once expressed the heartwarming sentiment that “the way you make a great joke is to start with a deep truth.” In that case, what is truthful about mocking oppressed minorities?  

  

For a seemingly harmless art, comedy certainly involves a lot of punching. *Punchlines*—the crux of any good joke—imply that someone is always at the sharp end of a metaphorical sword, and that execution serves as the basis for public entertainment. Unfortunately, when sensitive topics such as race or LGBTQ+ identities are introduced, these jokes can feel like yet another punch in the face to populations already suffering from societal prejudices. Offensive humour often fails in its quest to shock and defy norms—precisely because it plays into oft-repeated stereotypes and thus doesn’t break the status quo, only reestablishes it.  
  
  

It also begs the question: if comedians reserve the right to make potentially offensive jokes that target various (often marginalized) groups, then why should the same courtesy not be granted to those people-turned-punchlines when they choose to criticize them? By chastising so-called “keyboard warriors” and assuming victimhood, the likes of Rife and Chappelle ignore that their critics are simply engaging in the same freedom of speech that they so ardently claim to defend. For the sake of my word count, let us sidestep the philosophical debate of what should be protected under freedom of speech entirely. I often find myself perplexed in the face of a “cancelled” comedian’s tangents.  

  

As a writer, I understand the universal hustle for the ever elusive engagement, retention, and golden approval of the masses. Perhaps this is why I then struggle to sympathize with these comics who have essentially failed the most crucial test of public opinion: considering and adapting to one’s audience. Usually, a badly received joke would be cause to reassess and produce a funnier one, not to rage at your audience for not finding you very funny at all.  

  

Excuse my skepticism, but it seems awfully disingenuous—ludicrous even—to hear beloved celebrities complain about being “silenced”, only to secure yet another wildly popular Netflix special where they can further deplore their apparent censorship to an audience of millions. The irony of it all doesn’t escape me, although I wish these masters of comedy could see it for themselves.  

  

Ultimately, it is not up to me or anyone else to dictate what is and isn’t acceptable to say or laugh at. Attempting to define what constitutes “problematic” comedy easily leads us down a slippery slope, as the line in the sand of social norms shifts eternally without warning. When provocative comedians turn to hypocrisy to justify ill-received offensive jokes, we can be critical while still enjoying their more hilarious moments. So, I urge comics and readers alike to be considerate, as of course it’s easy to laugh when we don’t find ourselves at the butt of the joke.

**“I am a fraud” is a sentiment that has been reverberating inside me for ages.** Melodramatic? Fine, but I sure do feel like it sometimes, especially around February, the month of Lunar New Year—a holiday celebrated by Asians like me all over the world.  

  
When people ask about my ethnicity, I like to warn them that I am the most whitewashed Chinese boy anywhere in the Western hemisphere. Despite being sixteen years old, I have the chopstick dexterity of a toddler, and my pronunciation of words in “my language” is disgraceful.
I was a stubborn child growing up. I refused to speak a foreign language, but, in my opinion,  what was even the point? My family and loved ones were fluent and perfectly comfortable communicating with me in English, and they would simply continue to speak in their native language amongst each other—no harm, no foul. I was a picky eater of the highest degree. I rejected most foods, including many ethnic ones, but I survived just fine on whatever alternatives that I was being fed.At large family dinners, I would choose the safer, less adventurous options, while my family would feast on the wider spread. As a child, I was content living within the dam of Western customs. The feeling of “not being Chinese enough” hadn’t once crossed my mind.  

  
In late middle and early high school, I searched for my identity, as one does. At the same time, pieces of East Asian culture began to dominate social media, and quickly, I found myself in a crisis. For my entire life, I had sat back and watched as my family partook in cultural customs and experiences that I had excused myself from, or had minimal participation in. As a result, when confronted with questions that people assumed I’d have the answer to, I’d frantically stitch together a legitimate-sounding response using my blurred memories, trying to recall what other Chinese people in my life had done or said previously. I wasn’t able to speak authentically about this culture—**my culture.** The dam had broken. For the first time, the feeling of “not being Chinese enough” was real.  

  
My identity was never discouraged, and I am fortunate enough to have had very few encounters with xenophobia and the like. I never feel attacked for my identity, personally nor systemically. During Lunar New Year, I come back to terms with where I’m from and who I am. And now, after growing up, I can appreciate my cultural identity; albeit, later in life than the norm, but that's okay. There really is no reason for me to hold resentment. Who’s to say that I’m a fraud—it’s never too late for vindication.

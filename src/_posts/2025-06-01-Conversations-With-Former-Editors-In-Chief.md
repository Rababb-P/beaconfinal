**The helm of the Beacon has seen mighty captains in its former Editors-in-Chief.** Running Martingrove’s best newspaper undoubtedly provides unique insight and perspective. I wondered: How did the Beacon shape their post-high school lives, and what kind of advice would they give to us students and writers? So this past month, I interviewed three prior Editors. Read on to learn more!  

  

  **What is your name, which years were you involved with the Beacon, and what are you studying?**  

     

Skyler: Skyler Xiang. I was involved with the Beacon all four years of high school, first as a writer for copy in Grades 9-10, then as Editor-in-Chief in Grades 11-12. I graduated from MCI in 2024, and now have just finished my first year of Honours Urban Planning at the University of Waterloo.  

  

  Siya: I’m Siya Sahni, I was involved with the Beacon all four years [2019-2023], on the Layout Team in Grades 9 and 10 and as an Editor-in-Chief in Grades 11 and 12. I’m at [the] Schulich [School of Business, at York University], and am specializing in finance.  

     

Sanaa: I’m Sanaa Mahmud, and I was part of the Beacon from 2018-2022 where I had various roles including Columnist, Editor, and Editor-in-Chief. I’m currently going into my fourth year at the University of Toronto, pursuing a double major in Immunology and Human Biology.  

  

**What was/were the most valuable learning experience(s) from the Beacon you have taken with you  into your post-Beacon life? How did leading the paper shape your communication skills?**  

  

  Skyler: Definitely, my time management improved. There are many moving parts to getting an issue out on time, and coordinating all the different departments was formative in developing my organizational skills.  

      

As for my communication skills, I learned to be clear and concise with my instructions, both verbal and written, because no one wants to waste a whole lunch period over an announcement that could have been an email!  

  

Siya: Being an Editor-in-Chief meant being mindful of many moving pieces and managing a lot of different teams. Because of that, I got really good at communicating, but not over-communicating. The ability to be precise comes in really handy today, when I’m talking to actual professionals - they really appreciate that level of clarity.  

  

  Sanaa: The amazing collaboration between students. For every issue, I got to work with lots of talented people who had different skill sets relative to my own. I loved being able to see what others could create, and learned the importance of coming together with my fellow students to make some amazing Beacon issues that were representative of many different perspectives! In terms of communication skills, leading the Beacon definitely helped me learn how to manage a big group of people and make sure that everyone felt important and seen.  

      

**What advice would you give to current and aspiring student journalists/writers?**  

  


   Skyler: Write, write, write. Read, read, read. Write what you know, also - I find that the words come naturally when I write personal essays, which are really just extended diary entries.  

     

Siya: It’s truly important to be passionate about what you’re writing. I remember a point where we would brainstorm article ideas and assign them [to the writers], and we noticed that there wasn’t much passion coming through. But when we gave them a bit more freedom, and they did their own research, I was really shocked to see what the results were. I think that also translates over the long term. Really make sure you enjoy what you’re doing or planning on doing.  

  

Sanaa: My advice would be that if you enjoy writing and journalism, don’t stop pursuing it!  

  

  Look for different opportunities to practice writing and to show off your skills. Try to get involved in whatever you can in order to further your interests (and to make you happy of course!) Also, don’t get caught up in what other people around you are doing; just focus on yourself and making the most of your own experiences!  

      

**Has your time in student journalism influenced how you consume media today?**  

  

  Skyler: 100%. Understanding how to recognize fake news, bias, AI-written trash, and the like is so crucial. In an age where lunatics are trying to defund the CBC, and newspapers are being bought up by private companies to advance their own interest, it is crucial that we are able to differentiate between journalism and propaganda. Use your critical thinking skills! That's what English class is all about. In this era of AI-driven anti-intellectualism, it is so important to be able to analyze text and develop your own opinion about what it means. Don't let ChatGPT take away the ability to think for yourself.  

      

Siya: Yeah. I remember, even with the Beacon, it was very different what we put out on our Instagram compared to what the article was - I remember it was mostly clickbait. And then when you read the whole article, you actually absorbed the whole meaning. This was one of my biggest takeaways about media: don’t just focus on the flashy headlines. Actually go and read the article.  

  

Sanaa: Yes, I think it has! I definitely have a deeper understanding and appreciation of what it takes to create a piece of media like a news article or full newspaper issue, especially how complex the process is from start to finish. I also think that it’s important to realize that what the media presents to us is reflective of who’s making it, and to consider that there are multiple perspectives to every story.  

  

It’s so wonderful to hear from our former Editors. As a graduating student, their emphasis on passion in what we create and critical thinking of what we consume particularly struck me- I believe its importance only increases as we grow into this world.

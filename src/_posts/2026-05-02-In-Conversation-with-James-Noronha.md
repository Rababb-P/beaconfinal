**It’s not every day I find myself talking to someone with a clear passion for their work and alumni status at MCI but, I would argue Mr. Noronha isn't the average Joe.**

  

In 96’ James Noronha graduated from Martingrove CI, the very same windowless school with just as much heart. In grade 13, he was the president of SAC, well liked by peers, and an active member of several clubs, including MUN, debate, and drama. And ever since, he’s become a frequent attendee of alumni events.

  

Since graduating, he has worked in volunteer management with the government of Ontario, taught at a school for children with special needs, volunteered with NGOs promoting sports play for children affected by armed conflicts, worked with Special Olympics Ontario for nearly two decades which aids people with intellectual disabilities in accessing sports through empowerment and inclusion. There, he became the program director, involved with 25 thousand athletes, 11 thousand volunteers, and oversaw 40 staff who he worked with. That is to say that he has definitely done a lot since he was a high schooler. Currently, Mr. Noronha works at PooranLaw where he focuses specifically on disability law.

  

The following is what we discussed during our interview. As we spoke I quickly realized that his experiences in high school, volunteering, and working are all heavily interconnected. As he puts it, there's  “something about [our] school that far outlives the building.”

  

He began by reflecting on his high school years, explaining that some of his fondest memories stem from his time at MCI. He said it “gave [him] a platform... a voice.” He specifically appreciated the sheer amount of clubs at MCI, something that's stayed consistent over the years. He recalled that there was always something to do or be a part of. His graduation year remains really tight, and within five or so phone calls you could probably reach out to at least 50% of his graduating class. To Noronha, the building was a “home away from home“ and by the clear conviction in his voice, I believe to him it truly was.

  

MCI instilled a “growing and learning mindset” that followed him into post-secondary. He committed to the University of Toronto as a human biology major. To him it felt the natural thing to do, he liked helping people, spent a lot of time volunteering, and was good at science. Though very early on he realized that it was not the perfect fit. In part, he credits how much he enjoyed his time at MCI as one of the reasons the adjustment for him was so major. In high school he felt so involved in so many different things, and university felt a lot more rigid. So in second year he went about taking a diverse range of courses, as many that sounded engaging and honestly emulated the stimulation he got in high school. It was “more fun then head ever had.” After he graduated he continued to take plenty of classes from UofT here and there, from African studies to introductions to other planets. He found it not to really “feel like work [since he] like[ed] it.”

  

There's a definite interconnectedness I noticed throughout the interview. A lot of his experiences influence one another and he emphasized being inquisitive and allowing yourself to feel "unbalanced" so long as you're taking on the opportunities and passions you really want to pursue. Similarly, he explained he had never had a job he had not liked. During his time with the Special Olympics he felt really fulfilled by the good work he was doing and the positive impact it was having on so many people. As with many things, during the COVID-19 pandemic things changed. When it hit Mr. Noronha was primarily concerned with working to improve vaccine access, education, and management. Then his sister suggested he look into TMU’s new Lincoln Alexander School of Law; he found the focus on improving access to justice for the underserved really resonated with him - it was very similar to the work he was already doing and seemed like a natural shift. The fact he could continue to serve people with differing abilities but simply from a different angle was really appealing as he got to continue to “put people first” but in a different way.

  

To end off the interview I asked him what was one thing he would recommend to current high school students. Though he had not initially seen himself as a lawyer, looking at his story now it almost seems like it was predestined and in high school life can definitely feel just the opposite. He recommended that we “keep [our] options open and don't close doors until [we] have to,” emphasizing that we need to let what were most captivated by guide us and that we should do things so we don't find ourselves ever wondering what we “missed.”
